Listening and Spoken Language (LSL)
Emphasizes Speech Communication , optimal use of amplified residual hearing, and the development of speechreading
Full time, consistent use of amplification is essential.
Goal: understand and produce spoken language AUTOMATICALLY & SPONTANEOUSLY in the context of real communication

Slide 5

LSL: Auditory Verbal Therapy
Develop spoken language through listening by following the stages and sequence of typical development
Aggressive audiological management
Implemented in a clinic program or home setting
Parents/Caregivers are present and actively participating
Develop skills necessary for successful mainstreaming in school and integration into the hearing community
AVEd
Continuum of Services under one roof with specially trained staff

Slide 6

LSL: Oral/Auditory Oral
An approach that teaches a child to use his/her remaining hearing through amplification and the use of speechreading/natural gestures/visual cues to aid the child’s understanding of language.
Goal
To develop spoken language through listening and visual cues.
To develop spoken language and communication skills necessary for school success and integration into the hearing community.

Slide 7

Cued speech
Uses phonemically-based hand gestures to supplement speechreading
Talker speaks while cueing the message
Individually, the hand signals are uninterpretable They are used to distinguish viseme members
Has been adapted to more than 60 languages

Slide 8

Manual Visual Systems
American Sign Language (ASL)
Amplification and CI are not critical
Access to adults fluent in ASL
Bilingual-Bicultural
Children learn ASL as the first/primary language, and English is taught as a second language in written form
8

Slide 9

Total Communication
Uses an English-based sign language system which can include speech, speech reading, finger spelling, natural gestures and the use of residual hearing.
GOALS
To provide a bridge to the development of spoken language in the very young child.
To provide communication between the child and his/her family, teachers and peers using sign language.
To support integration into both the hearing and the Deaf communities.
The child develops language through speech reading, listening and exposure to a combination of speech and sign-based systems in English order (SEE II)

Selection of a communication mode
There is much debate about which to choose
Ultimate decision depends upon the child
Some evidence suggests aural/oral communication helps achieve better speech and language performance, and literacy development
Cochlear implants have provided many with ability to acquire spoken language

Slide 36

Speech Characteristics With Hearing Loss
Speech Characteristics
Historically, children with mild to moderate hearing loss often had intelligible speech, with the predominant speech errors being either:
the misarticulation of single consonants or consonant blends
Sounds most commonly in error were the affricates, fricatives, and blends.
Given these types of errors, the use of standard articulation tests is appropriate.
With the advances in access to hearing technology, degree of hearing loss is often not a factor for outcomes in speech development

Articulation testing
Often conventional articulation tests are used to assess segmental speech skills. Most common:
Goldman-Fristoe (GFTA-3)
Test of Minimal Articulation Competence (T-MAC)
Arizona Articulation and Phonology Scale- 4
Tests designed for children with significant hearing loss:
Phonetic Level and the Phonological Level Speech Evaluation
CID Phonetic Inventory
Speech Intelligibility Evaluation (SPINE)
Beginners’ Intelligibility Test (BIT)

Slide 42

Assessing articulation skills
Speech Assessment
Speech intelligibility measures are typically based on recorded speech samples, with two different ways to obtain the intelligibility rating.
First, one or more listeners can be asked to write down the words they hear, and the examiner can determine the percentage of words accurately identified
Alternatively, listeners can be asked to listen to the entire speech passage and rate how recognizable the words are in the passage

Speech therapy
Goals of a speech development program can include:
Increase vocalizations that have appropriate timing characteristics, require several vocal tract movements.
Expand phonetic and phonemic repertoires.
Establish link between audition and speech production.
Increase speech intelligibility.

Speech Characteristics of Acquired Hearing Loss
Extremely variable effects
Usually the degree of hearing loss has to be very significant, especially in the high frequencies
Decline in intelligibility is gradual and due to loss of auditory feedback

Slide 45

Cochlear Implants and speech production
Children who receive cochlear implants, especially those who receive one at an early age, typically acquire better speech intelligibility and segmental and suprasegmental speaking abilities than children who have profound hearing loss and who use hearing aids.
Typically acquire speech sounds in the same order as children with normal hearing
