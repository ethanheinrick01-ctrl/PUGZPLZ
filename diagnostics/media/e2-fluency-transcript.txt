Week 10: Fluency
Original teaching narration based on the supplied slides; lecture transcript not yet available.

A smooth sentence can hide an important assessment problem. The speaker may have changed words, avoided a situation, or asked someone else to speak. Fluency assessment therefore needs both the speech sample and the person's experience. Stuttering can include repetitions, prolongations, blocks, and broken words. Cluttering can involve a rapid or irregular rate, atypical prosody, and reduced clarity in connected speech. The two can coexist. Fast speech alone is not a complete diagnosis.

Start with the history and choose samples that represent the person's life. Ask about onset, family history, variation, and prior therapy. Also ask about the person's reactions. Identify situations that are easier or harder. Gather speech across more than one session and setting. Conversation, narrative, and reading can place different demands on the speaker. A short successful clinic exchange cannot stand in for all of those situations. Keep the context attached to the sample so that a change in performance remains interpretable.

Now follow the counting rule. In the course method, a repeated unit is counted as one repetition event, rather than counting each repeated attempt as a separate event. Suppose a sample contains one hundred words and ten disfluency events. The total disfluency index is ten percent. If six of those events are repetitions, the repetition index is six percent of the words, while repetitions make up sixty percent of the disfluencies. Same six events, different denominator, different question.

Frequency is only one dimension. Measure duration, describe the types, and consider speaking rate with and without disfluencies. Then look beyond the speech stream. Associated movements, physical responses, avoidance, embarrassment, and participation can change the meaning of an apparently low count. A person who speaks less or relies on a partner may reduce observable disfluencies while still experiencing a substantial problem. Ask what the speaker is doing and what the speaker is giving up.

The slides list numerical considerations for stuttering: a total index of ten percent, relevant type indexes of three percent, and an average duration of one second. Learn these as the course's assessment considerations, alongside the prevalent types, secondary behaviors, and client or parent concern. They are not a substitute for integrating the whole assessment. For suspected cluttering, compare structured articulation performance with spontaneous speech, and consider rate, prosody, coarticulation, and awareness.

Finally, examine responsiveness to support. Stimulability asks how performance changes when an appropriate technique is tried, starting at a simpler level and increasing complexity. Show the change in the task and the response, rather than assuming one good attempt settles the diagnosis. The assessment should explain the pattern, its context, and its impact. Counts describe the sample. The combined evidence explains the person's needs.
