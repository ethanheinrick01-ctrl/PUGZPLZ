Follow the child’s communication

Two children can earn the same language score and still need different evaluations. The score tells you how they performed on that task. It does not explain the entire communication problem. Begin with the child's history, hearing, language experience, and the family's concern. Then choose procedures that can answer that concern. Screening asks whether a deeper assessment is needed. A comprehensive evaluation asks what the pattern means and what should happen next.

Language has five connected domains: semantics, syntax, morphology, pragmatics, and phonology. Meaning, sentence structure, word structure, social use, and the sound system work together. The assessment must also fit the child's development. With a preverbal child, look for gestures, intentional communication, responses, and shared attention. Play and imitation can show foundations that a picture naming task would miss. As language develops, add word combinations, grammatical forms, more complex directions, and narratives. Observing a familiar caregiver can reveal abilities that a child does not show with an unfamiliar examiner.

The lecture describes fewer than fifty expressive words or no word combinations at age two as an increased risk signal. Risk is not a diagnosis, and it does not tell you the child's future by itself. Effective nonverbal communication, strong comprehension, and age appropriate speech sound accuracy are favorable signs in the course material. Evaluate the whole pattern. A standardized test contributes a comparison with its normative sample. Informal observations and targeted tasks reveal how language works in daily life. Dynamic assessment adds teaching and retesting to investigate learning.

A useful language sample varies people, activities, and contexts. Record it so that you can examine the actual productions. For morphology, separate free morphemes from bound morphemes. In the simple example dogs, dog stands alone and the plural ending attaches to it. Mean length of utterance is the total number of counted morphemes divided by the number of included utterances. If a sample has one hundred twenty morphemes across forty utterances, its mean length is three. That is a worked arithmetic example, not a diagnosis or an age equivalent.

Now change the lens without changing the sample. Semantics asks about word meanings, diversity, substitutions, and word finding. Syntax asks how phrases, clauses, and sentences are built. Pragmatics asks how the child manages communication with another person in that situation. Narrative analysis examines how a story is organized and expressed. These are complementary analyses. Compare the formal results with the family's observations. When findings disagree, investigate the reason. The goal is a defensible account of the child's communication, not a label produced by one number.
