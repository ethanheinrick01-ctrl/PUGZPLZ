A speaking valve changes the route of exhaled air. A tracheostomy provides an opening into the trachea through the neck. Normally, exhaled air passes through the larynx, where vibrating vocal folds produce sound. The valve opens for inhalation through the tube. It closes during exhalation, redirecting air around the tube and through the upper airway. That redirected air can support voice.

An exhalation route must remain open. The cuff must be completely deflated before the valve is placed. An inflated cuff can block the space needed for exhaled air to pass around the tube. But deflating the cuff is not enough by itself. The patient also needs adequate upper-airway airflow and must tolerate the change. If airflow is inadequate, do not place the valve. Further assessment is needed.

The deck organizes evaluation into five steps. First, check alertness, ability to follow directions, breathing comfort and oxygen stability. Second, assess secretions, cough and clearance. Third, work with respiratory therapy to deflate the cuff completely and observe the response. Fourth, check airflow through the mouth or nose and look for obstruction. Fifth, if appropriate, try the valve under supervision while monitoring breathing, oxygen, voice and comfort.

Audible speech does not override breathing difficulty. Remove the valve for difficulty breathing, falling oxygen levels, increased effort, stridor, anxiety or distress, or inability to exhale. Document the baseline, cuff response, airflow, voice and respiratory tolerance. The deck’s successful example leads to initial speech-language pathology and respiratory therapy supervision, followed by gradual progression according to tolerance and facility protocol. That recommendation follows the observed response; it is not automatic for every patient.
