Separate language from the wider pattern

Fluent speech is not the same thing as intact language. After a neurological event, a person may speak easily while struggling to understand, or understand relatively well while producing short, effortful phrases. Begin with the person's earlier communication, the event or course of change, current function, hearing and vision, medications, and cultural and linguistic context. Different disorders can occur together. The evaluation has to describe that combination.

Aphasia is an acquired language impairment caused by brain injury. In the course comparison, Wernicke's aphasia has fluent output but substantial comprehension difficulty, with possible jargon and word errors. Broca's aphasia has effortful, nonfluent, often telegraphic output with relatively better auditory comprehension. These are teaching profiles, not rules that every patient matches perfectly. Assess spoken comprehension and expression, reading, writing, naming, repetition, and functional communication. Fluent output alone cannot establish that the message is accurate or understood.

Keep the error terms precise. Anomia means difficulty finding a word. Circumlocution means talking around the target, such as calling a key the thing that opens the door. A neologism is a created, nonsensical word. Perseveration is the inappropriate continuation of a previous response or behavior. Agrammatism concerns grammatical accuracy and can produce telegraphic speech. Record the behavior before assigning its label, and examine how the errors change across tasks.

Right hemisphere damage can affect attention, perception, organization, insight, prosody, and social communication as well as language. A person may build a grammatical sentence yet miss its implied meaning or emotional tone. The two hemispheres contribute complementary functions. Traumatic brain injury can also combine language and cognitive difficulties, including attention, memory, judgment, and organization. The assessment therefore reaches beyond naming or sentence production. Medical readiness and the person's level of responsiveness affect what can meaningfully be assessed.

The Glasgow Coma Scale describes eye, verbal, and motor responses. The Rancho levels describe patterns of cognitive and behavioral functioning. The Glasgow Outcome Scale addresses a different question: overall outcome. These tools are not interchangeable, and a single communication assessment does not establish brain death. In dementia assessment, trace the onset and progression, combine history with cognitive and language findings, and examine functional potential. The course's early, intermediate, and advanced stages chiefly describe an Alzheimer's type progression. Across these populations, choose the assessment for the clinical question, interpret the whole pattern, and explain the functional implications.
