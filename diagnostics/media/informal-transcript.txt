Three methods, three questions
Clinical observation watches and listens: what is the client doing? Informal assessment sets up a non-standardized task: what can the client do on this task? Dynamic assessment teaches and examines the response: what changes with support, and can learning be used again? You can observe communication during a standardized assessment. The setting alone does not decide which method the observation represents.

Compare support and transfer
Begin with an independent attempt. Teach using models, cues, feedback and practice. Retest with reduced support or a new task. The narrative example teaches character, setting, problem, action and outcome, then examines a new story. Repeating a language sample without instruction is not dynamic assessment. Keep the amount of help and the transfer evidence visible.

Language and narrative levels
Language sample analysis examines form, semantic intent, use, rate and sequencing across representative contexts. Semantic analysis looks at vocabulary variety, unusual substitutions, empty words and word finding. Establish story comprehension before interpreting retelling. Macrostructure is the story’s organization. Microstructure is grammar, vocabulary, syntax and cohesion. Higher-level language includes inference, motivation, causality and perspective.

Observe play without overclaiming
The class activities examine cause and effect, intention and play. Watch whether an action is repeated to obtain an outcome, how the child communicates a purpose, and whether symbolic play appears. Motor, sensory and access barriers can limit task performance. One unsuccessful toy interaction cannot establish absent understanding. Use the particular supplied checklist in its own context; do not merge its developmental ages with unrelated speech-sound norms.
