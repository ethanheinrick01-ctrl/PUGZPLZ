Follow the air, but keep airflow separate from sound. For oral speech sounds, the soft palate and pharyngeal walls help separate the oral and nasal routes. Incomplete closure can allow air to escape through the nose. Speech may sound overly nasal, and pressure consonants may sound weak. Nasal sounds normally use the nasal route, so the goal is not to close it during every sound.

Now separate three observations. Hypernasality is excessive nasal resonance, especially on vowels. Nasal emission is air escaping through the nose during pressure consonants. Compensatory articulation concerns the place where a sound is produced, such as a glottal stop instead of an oral consonant. Hyponasality is reduced nasal resonance and may suggest obstruction. These are different findings, even when more than one is present.

Build the evaluation from several kinds of evidence. Ask about cleft history, palate surgery, feeding, hearing, speech development and the family’s concerns. Examine the palate, uvula, visible throat structures, tongue and lips. A bifid uvula may suggest a submucous cleft, but it does not establish the diagnosis. The oral examination alone cannot diagnose velopharyngeal insufficiency. Add connected speech, syllables, pressure-rich sentences and sustained vowels. Nasopharyngoscopy can show closure during speech.

Document structures, movement, resonance, nasal emission, speech production and intelligibility. Then match management to the explanation. A structural problem may need surgery or a prosthetic device. Neuromotor dysfunction may require medical evaluation and individualized care. Learned compensatory placement and learned phoneme-specific nasal emission may respond to speech therapy. The essential distinction is the cause of the error, not simply whether the speech sounds nasal.
