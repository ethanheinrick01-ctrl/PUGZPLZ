Week 10: Dysphagia
Original teaching narration based on the supplied slides; lecture transcript not yet available.

Watch the food, but also watch what protects the airway. Swallowing has four phases. Oral preparation forms a bolus. Oral transit moves that bolus toward the back of the mouth. During the pharyngeal phase, the soft palate closes off the nasal route, the larynx rises, and several airway protections work together while the bolus enters the esophagus. Esophageal transport then carries it toward the stomach. This is a slowed schematic of coordinated events, not four steps that last the same amount of time.

Development changes the feeding task. An infant's sucking pattern is not the same skill as a toddler bringing a spoon to the mouth. The course table traces changes in head control, tongue and jaw movement, chewing, cup use, and self-feeding. Compare the child's pattern with development and history, rather than treating one isolated age as a diagnosis. Some slide entries are abbreviated or contain typos; the guide identifies the textbook clarification instead of making you memorize the wrong entry.

For a pediatric evaluation, begin with medical and feeding history, dietary restrictions, and the caregiver's account. Then observe the child's state. A sleeping child, a drowsy child, and an alert child present different conditions for assessment. So do agitation and crying. Breathing and alertness matter to readiness. Observe sucking, reflexes, oral structures, movement, and coordination. Nonnutritive sucking uses no food or liquid; nutritive sucking includes intake and is slower in the course description. Neither a single movement nor a single observation answers the whole feeding question.

Adult assessment also begins before the first bite. Review neurological, medical, cognitive, and feeding status, including dietary limitations. Examine the lips, tongue, jaw, sensation, and coordination. In the course's bedside assessment, a client who is not alert, or whose known swallowing problem makes an oral trial dangerous, should not receive the test food or liquid. A cough, choking, or a wet voice raises concern. An apparently quiet swallow does not establish that the airway is safe.

Instrumental studies answer questions that the clinical examination may leave unresolved. A videofluoroscopic swallow study uses moving X-ray images to examine bolus flow and swallowing function. An endoscopic evaluation uses a flexible scope to examine pharyngeal and laryngeal structures, secretions, and findings around the swallow. The views and limits differ. The slides call videofluoroscopy preferred, but that is not a universal rule that makes endoscopy the wrong choice. Select the assessment around the question, the person, and the clinical circumstances.

Bring the evidence back together. Identify the phase or function of concern, the conditions under which it occurs, and the additional information needed. Posture, equipment, texture, pacing, and the feeding environment are assessment considerations in the course, not interchangeable fixes. A strategy needs a reason and an observed effect. The central task is to connect history, observed function, safety, and the appropriate next assessment step.
