Screening is the first question
A speech sound screening quickly identifies people who may need further assessment. It is not an in-depth evaluation or a diagnosis. A comprehensive assessment considers relevant history, oral structure and function, hearing, perception, single words and connected speech. Speech sound difficulty may involve perception, planning, articulation or phonology. Match evidence to the suspected mechanism.

Compare word and connected speech
Single-word tests often sample a sound in only one context and may omit vowels or linguistic varieties. Connected speech adds error consistency, patterns, rate and intelligibility. Analyze the number, type, form and position of errors. Stimulability asks whether the client can produce an erred sound correctly with assistance; it provides prognostic information. It does not automatically erase a disorder.

Norms are one part of diagnosis
Developmental sound acquisition, conversational acquisition, consonant-cluster development and occurrence frequency answer different questions. Use the relevant table and context. Do not diagnose from developmental norms alone. Consider intelligibility, stimulability, perception, error patterns, social and academic impact, history and language. Phonological processing concerns thinking about, remembering or manipulating sounds; speech sound production concerns how the words are spoken.

Record the production you hear
The Week six GFTA activity requires real-time error recording. Use IPA for substitutions, a dash for an omission, diacritics or notes for a distortion, and N R for no response. Follow the appropriate test manual’s prompts and scoring. No response is not automatically an omitted phoneme. GFTA findings contribute to a broader profile alongside connected speech, hearing and oral examination, history and other relevant measures.
