Build a complete evaluation
A complete evaluation combines history, interview, direct assessment, analysis, report and results sharing. Hearing screening or recent audiological findings help address hearing as a contributor. The setting changes the immediate question: school evaluation examines educational impact; an outpatient evaluation considers functional outcomes; acute care may prioritize a targeted time-sensitive assessment.

Start broad, then clarify
Open the interview with rapport, purpose and structure. Ask open-ended questions about the main concern, onset, change, consistency and everyday impact. Follow with specific examples and relevant developmental, medical, hearing and language history. Listen actively and clarify contradictions. A family’s priorities help shape the evaluation. A history is a starting point, and informant reports still need interpretation.

Integrate before diagnosing
Consider findings in the context of the history. A normal vocabulary score can coexist with difficulty conversing with peers. A low timed score can coexist with effective compensatory communication. Do not force a diagnosis from conflicting evidence. Further testing can accompany a diagnosis and plan. Recommendations specify goals, setting, frequency and session duration, based on the supported needs.

Report the clinical meaning
A useful report includes identifying information, referral reason, history, procedures, results by domain, clinical impressions and recommendations. Explain quantitative findings and qualitative function. In the conference, use clear language, recognize strengths, allow time for questions, and close with next steps. Protect identifying health information when sharing records. A list of scores without functional interpretation is an unfinished clinical argument.
